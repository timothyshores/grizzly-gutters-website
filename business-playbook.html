Why gutters, and why we stay in gutters

The plan is to go deep on gutters before going wide. Not because gutters are special, but because the second gutter site cost less to build than the first, and the third will cost less than the second.

Same layout, same objections, same questions, same trust signals, same photos of the same kind of work. That only compounds if we stay in one trade long enough to build the template. Jumping to roofing after two sites throws away the thing that makes us fast and cheap.

Grow by geography, not by trade. Salt Lake first, then Ogden, Provo, Park City, St. George. Then Boise, Vegas, Denver. Same pitch, same site, new phone book — and every call gets easier because we can say "we do this for gutter companies" instead of "we build websites."

Other trades come later — roofing, landscaping, HVAC, concrete, fencing are the identical pitch with different photos. But not until the gutter version is closing consistently.

The Angi angle (the best question on a cold call)

You know how shared leads work. The gutter-specific numbers: $20–70 per lead, sold to three to eight companies at once, plus a membership fee and a monthly ad minimum. He pays whether or not he wins the job.

Ask: "What are you paying Angi or Thumbtack right now?" Most useful question you can ask, because it's the sore spot in this trade and he'll give you a real number.

Do his math out loud. $40 a lead, closing one in six, is $240 in lead fees per job before he buys a screw. Most contractors have never run it.

Then land it: "a lead off your own website is yours alone. Nobody else got it." That's the product — not a website, leads he doesn't race anyone for.

Booking appointments — the honest answer

This one surprised us. The research argues against building it.
Homeowners who call83% still prefer phoning a contractor over booking online
Prefer online bookingOnly 12%
Phone vs formPhone leads convert 10–15x better than web forms

A gutter estimate can't be booked like a haircut. You have to measure the house before quoting. So a calendar slot isn't really booking a job — it's booking a visit that still needs a phone conversation to qualify. Skip that conversation and you send a truck to a house you can't actually quote.

So the biggest win isn't a booking calendar. It's calling back fast. That's what the auto-text and instant lead alert already do, and it's why they come first on the ladder.
That said, booking is still worth offering — as a fallback, not the main event. Roughly 15–20% of people won't call: they're browsing at 10pm, or they're younger and just don't phone strangers. A "pick a time" button captures those instead of losing them.

Cal.com's free tier does this at $0 — unlimited bookings, email and text reminders, drops into the site we already built. Nothing to host, nothing to maintain.

What we will NOT do is compete with Jobber. Jobber is real scheduling software for contractors — $29/month to start, $99/month once you want automatic text reminders and two-way texting. If a client wants a full job calendar with dispatching, that's Jobber's job, and the right answer is to say so. We're not going to underprice a real product with a worse version of it.

Why we won't sell an AI that calls leads

It sounds perfect — an AI dials the lead in ten seconds and books the job. It's illegal without consent our forms don't collect, so we're not offering it. Here's the short version so you can answer confidently if a client asks.

The FCC ruled in February 2024 that AI voices count as "artificial" voices under the TCPA — the anti-robocall law. Calling a cell phone with one needs written consent collected up front, with a checkbox and specific wording. A "get a free estimate" button doesn't come close.

The fine is $500 per call, up to $1,500 if it's willful. No cap. And because we'd build the same form for every client, one mistake wouldn't hit one contractor — it'd hit all of them, and us along with them.

If a client asks for it, tell him the truth: "That's regulated. It needs written consent your form doesn't collect, and it's up to $1,500 a call if it's wrong." Saying that makes you the guy who knows the rules — which is worth more than the feature.

And we don't need it. An instant text hits the same five-minute window with a fraction of the risk. Courts have held texts aren't "voice" under this law. Same speed, no exposure.

Can we track phone calls from the site?

Yes, and it's a real upsell. It's called call tracking, and it works like this: instead of putting the contractor's real number on the site, we put a second number there that forwards straight to his phone. He answers normally and never notices the difference — but now every call through the site is counted, timestamped, and recordable.

Why it matters: right now we can prove the form works but we're blind on calls, and 83% of homeowners call instead of filling out the form. Which means the thing that proves our value is the thing we currently can't measure.

Cost: about $1.15/month for the number plus roughly a penny a minute. Not a hard build. This makes the monthly report dramatically more convincing — "your site drove 14 calls and 3 form leads last month" instead of "3 leads."

One warning: a tracking number has to be handled carefully with Google Business Profile, since Google wants your number consistent everywhere. There's a standard way to do it. Tim's problem, not a call-blocker.

We can also track visitors — how many people saw the site, and whether they came from Google, Facebook, or the link you texted them. Free, and a small build.

What is Jobber (and when to hand off)

Jobber is scheduling and invoicing software for contractors. Job calendar, crew dispatch, quotes, invoices, payments. $29/month to start, $99/month once you want automatic text reminders and two-way texting. Housecall Pro is the same idea at ~$59/month.

This is not our competition and we shouldn't pretend it is. If a client wants a real job calendar with dispatching and invoicing, tell him to buy Jobber. Saying "that's what Jobber is for, it's about $30 a month" makes you the guy who gives straight answers instead of the guy who sells whatever's asked for.

Our lane is getting the lead in the door fast. Jobber's lane is managing the work after it's booked. Different problems.

Show clients their numbers every month

Every paying client gets a monthly lead report. Not because they ask — because it's the cheapest thing that keeps them paying.

Remember: price is only the 6th reason clients leave. The top two are weak guidance and poor communication. Clients don't quit because $150 is too much. They quit because it feels like nothing's happening.

The report is proof something happened. How many leads came in, what each one wanted, how it compares to last month. The form already captures the service and how urgent it was.

Once there's a few months of history it becomes a sales weapon: "your site brought in 11 leads last month — at your job values that's real pipeline, for $150." And then it's the cold-call opener for the next client, in any trade: "our contractor sites average X leads a month." That beats "we build websites" by a mile.

Start manual. A count and a short email beats no report and beats building software nobody's paying for.

The one thing that will kill a monthly client

43% of all churn happens in the first 90 days — before the work has had time to prove itself. And the #1 reason clients leave isn't price, it's feeling like nothing is happening.

So the first month matters more than the next twelve. Get him a real, visible lead in week one and say something about it. A client who sees one lead come through stays for years — retainer clients average 56 months. A client who hears nothing for 30 days cancels.

Your job after the sale isn't nothing. Text him when a lead comes in. "Did that one turn into a job?" That single question is most of retention.

Why volume, not big fish

Ten clients at $500 beats one at $5,000, because the ten are easy yeses and the one takes three months of meetings and a proposal deck.

And gutters aren't special. Roofing, landscaping, HVAC, concrete, fencing — same pitch, same site, different photos. Once the gutter version works, it's a template we point at any local trade.

The websites are the door, not the business. One-time work means starting from zero every month. The recurring $150 is what actually compounds — month 13 builds on month 12 instead of replacing it. Sell the site to get in the door; the monthly is the real product.

If someone doesn't pay

Take the $500 up front. It's not a big ask — it's less than he pays Angi in a slow month. Money before launch, monthly auto-billed on a card. The best way to not get stiffed is to not extend credit to a stranger.

If it happens anyway, we have real leverage and none of it requires a lawyer:

1. Turn the site off. We own the hosting. Non-payment, site goes dark. Instant and completely defensible — we stopped providing a service he stopped paying for.
2. Stop lead delivery. Remove his email from the form. Takes 30 seconds.
3. The domain stays in his name — on purpose. We never hold his property hostage. That's what would create legal problems for us.

What we don't do: sell his leads to another gutter company. Not squeamishness — those leads aren't his property to seize, they're the homeowner's personal info. A guy typed his number into what he thought was one company's form. Handing him to three competitors is a use he never agreed to, and he didn't do anything wrong — the contractor did.

Also: contractors in one metro all talk. "They sold my customer list over a billing dispute" is the story that ends this business. Turning the site off is faster leverage anyway.
A chargeback is different, and turning the site off does not stop one. A chargeback is when he calls his bank and says "I didn't authorize this." The bank pulls the $500 back out of our account right away, plus about a $15 fee, before anyone asks us anything. The bank decides, not us. Shutting his site off is a fine consequence, but it doesn't get the money back.

What actually wins one is proof. We fight it by sending the bank the live site URL, the dated email where we told him it's live, and the payment page showing what he bought. That's why we always send a "you're live" email with the link — it's the receipt.

Brandon: if a client ever mentions disputing a charge, tell Tim the same day. There's a deadline to respond and missing it is an automatic loss. Realistically this is rare on a $500 site that visibly exists, but it's not zero.

Don't say the site is done before it's done

This already bit us once. Brandon told Salt Lake Gutter Guys the site was finished a day before it actually was. Tim had other things going that day, and the client came back with "wait, you said it was done."

Not a big deal, and easy to avoid from here. The fix isn't to promise less — it's that the demo goes live before the pitch, every time. Both current sites are live right now, so this shape works: build it, confirm the link loads, then call.

Being able to say "it's already built, here's the link" is the strongest thing about this pitch. It just has to be true when you say it. Text Tim to confirm the link is live before a first call — takes him ten seconds.
